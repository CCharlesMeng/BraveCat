## Geometry cues established

- DOC describes Mitre Peak as rising **1,692 m directly from the water** with sheer rock walls. Preserve that abrupt mountain-to-water relationship; do not add a broad inhabited foothill, road, town, or developed waterfront between the peak and fiord.
- DOC describes the wider area as glacially carved, with U-shaped valleys opening into sheer rugged basins and peaks. UNESCO likewise identifies successive glaciation, towering cliffs, fjords, rocky coasts, lakes, and waterfalls as defining Te Wahipounamu landforms.
- The planned recognition silhouette is a steep, narrow, asymmetric pyramid with a clear summit and descending jagged shoulder, framed by much larger dark fiord walls. Keep it rock-faced with forested lower slopes, not a smooth, symmetric, heavily snow-covered fantasy cone.
- Keep the water long, calm, cool blue-gray, and vertically reflective. Fiord walls should descend strongly toward the water and layer into atmospheric distance rather than read as low lake hills.
- Lower slopes may carry restrained dense native forest. UNESCO identifies southern beech and podocarps as dominant cover across the World Heritage property; DOC describes the setting as exceptionally wet and rugged.
- Mist and faint natural cascades are plausible, but neither may erase the summit silhouette. Golden light should remain pale and humid rather than a saturated alpine sunset.
- A low-detail natural foreground rock shelf is a composition device for the future Portrait. It must remain visibly grounded but must not resemble a built viewing platform, paving, a marked stage, or an actual feature claimed by the sources.

## Companion evidence and depiction limits

- A kea at Piopiotahi is geographically plausible. DOC says kea occur from mountains to sea level across Te Waipounamu from Golden Bay to Fiordland, and specifically lists the Milford Track and Homer Tunnel area as places where they may be seen.
- UNESCO's official property description also states that kea live within Te Wahipounamu.
- Plausibility does not make the bird ordinary or guaranteed at the exact foreshore viewpoint. Keep it exclusive to the rare variant: exactly one small perched kea, under the plan's 6% frame-height ceiling, on a lateral natural rock outside the Portrait safe bounds.
- Use conservative species cues only: olive-green parrot proportions, a dark curved bill, closed wings, and a tapered tail. Keep any orange underwing hidden or extremely restrained in a calm perched pose.
- DOC classifies kea as endemic and Threatened–Nationally Endangered and identifies the species as an important taonga for Ngāi Tahu and other iwi. Do not caricature it as a pest or comic thief.
- Do not show feeding, food, rubbish, vehicles, buildings, human gear, or a human interaction. DOC explicitly warns that feeding is harmful and encourages dangerous habituation around human structures and cars.

## Naming and cultural cautions

- Te Rūnanga o Ngāi Tahu identifies the place as **Piopiotahi, or Milford Sound**, within **Te Rua-o-te-Moko**, and describes Piopiotahi as a treasured ancestral place over which eight Papatipu Rūnaka exercise mana whenua.
- Prefer **Piopiotahi / Milford Sound** or a product-wide form approved by cultural review. The locked ASCII destination ID remains `new-zealand-fiordland-milford-sound`; it is an implementation identifier, not cultural naming guidance.
- Do not state one origin of “Piopiotahi” as uncontested fact. DOC gives an association with the now-extinct piopio, while Te Rūnanga o Ngāi Tahu explicitly records multiple accounts, including a waka account and a Māui/piopio account.
- Ngāi Tahu's account places Piopiotahi within creation traditions concerning Tū Te Rakiwhānoa and Te Hamo. These narratives and associated place names are living cultural knowledge, not generic decorative motifs. Do not add adze, waka, pou, pounamu, kōwhaiwhai, or invented “Māori-inspired” symbols without mana-whenua-led art direction.
- Preserve source orthography and dialect rather than silently “correcting” Kāi Tahu forms. Any public-facing Māori copy, pronunciation, alternate name for Mitre Peak, or story summary remains pending cultural review.
- Do not use UNESCO or World Heritage emblems, marks, or implied endorsement. World Heritage status is factual context, not a branding asset.
